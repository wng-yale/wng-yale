Chapter VIII

Information and the Efficiency of the Capital Markets

How information becomes embedded in prices, the three forms of market efficiency, and the evidence for and against efficient markets.

I. Arbitrage and the Price of Risk

Chapters IV and V develop the concept of the Security Market Line: in a market at equilibrium, every security lies on that line, its expected return determined by its beta — the price of risk. But markets are seldom at equilibrium. News arrives and expectations diverge, and a security’s price departs from the line: for a time it is mispriced. The mechanism that restores it is arbitrage. When a stock trades below the line (its price too high for its risk) or above it (too low), investors who identify the discrepancy sell the overpriced security and buy the underpriced one, and their trading returns the price to the line. This chapter examines that mechanism more closely — how mispricings arise as information arrives, and how quickly and completely arbitrage removes them.

Arbitrage requires a search for mispriced securities. Arbitrageurs study cash flows, discount rates, and the firm-specific factors that affect the price of a single stock — the health of a chief executive, the reception of a product, a pending lawsuit. Opportunities for this “arbitrage in expectations” are uncommon, and they are brief once found, because speed is essential: once the buying begins, the price adjusts and the opportunity closes. The central question of the chapter is how well this mechanism works: how rapidly and how thoroughly are mispricings eliminated?

Informational Efficiency

Market efficiency is a statement about speed: how quickly relevant information becomes embedded in prices. The mechanism is a kind of invisible hand. An overpriced asset attracts short-sellers whose sales lower its price, and an underpriced one attracts buyers whose purchases raise it. The concept is informational efficiency, which concerns how fast news is reflected in prices; it is distinct from Pareto efficiency, which concerns how resources are allocated. Two factors determine the speed of adjustment: liquidity, since an illiquid market cannot react quickly, and psychology, since a crowd can over- or under-react when the meaning of the news is hard to judge.

Information once traveled only as fast as people did. In the eighteenth century a price change in Amsterdam reached London on the Thursday after the Monday it occurred — the time a fast messenger needed to cross the Channel. Today the same information reaches every market within seconds.

II. Regnault and the Value of Private Information

The idea that prices already reflect what is knowable is usually dated to the 1950s and 1960s. The idea was stated and tested nearly a century earlier, by a practitioner who worked in the market. Jules Regnault (1834–1894), a broker’s assistant in Second-Empire Paris, taught himself mathematics in a garret near the Bourse and in 1863 published Calcul des chances et philosophie de la bourse, arguably the first statistical theory of the stock market. He built the theory, tested it against data, and then applied it to become wealthy, retiring as a rentier with a fortune held mostly in bonds.

Regnault began with a direct question: who makes money in the market? For the ordinary investor, he argued, trading is a fair game at best; the only reliable profit belongs to those who know something the public does not.

“There is only one certain way of making a sure profit at the Stock Market, which is to only trade in guaranteed information unknown to the public … accessible only to a happy few, who is admitted to … those private meetings where the great political and financial decisions are plotted. This is the primary cause of the detrimental inequality which it is impossible to escape.”
— Jules Regnault, Calcul des chances (1863), §15

His example was close at hand: the French rente, the government bond whose price swung on decisions taken in ministries, where a civil servant “who takes advantage of his position and State secrets” held every advantage. The legendary example is Nathan Rothschild, whose courier network is said to have brought him word of Waterloo before London had it, allowing him to trade ahead of the news. In modern terms, Regnault’s insight is that markets are efficient with respect to public information but not necessarily with respect to private information, and the advantage belongs to those who know what the public does not yet know. If only private information is profitable, the relevant question is how quickly private information ceases to be private and is reflected in the price.

Consider what happens when private information becomes public. A major corporate event — a merger, a disaster, a rate decision — immediately changes expected cash flows, and the price changes with them. Union Carbide’s stock fell sharply within minutes of the news of the Bhopal chemical disaster, and stock prices respond to a Federal Reserve rate decision on the day it is announced. The standard method for measuring these responses is the event study, which lines up many such events at day zero and averages the cumulative abnormal return around them.

Figure 8.1. An event study of merger targets. The line is the cumulative abnormal return of firms that received a takeover bid, averaged and aligned on the announcement day (day 0). Roughly half of the total run-up occurs before the announcement, consistent with private information reaching the market ahead of the news, and the remainder arrives at once on the day itself. Afterward the return is flat, with no drift to trade on, consistent with the public news being fully reflected in the price. After Keown and Pinkerton, “Merger Announcements and Insider Trading Activity,” Journal of Finance (1981).

The pattern is consistent with an efficient market, with one qualification. Part of the adjustment occurs on the announcement day and none afterward; there is no drift, because no public news remains to trade on. Much of the run-up, however, occurs before the announcement, consistent with private information entering the price ahead of the news, as Regnault described, until the announcement makes the information public and the remaining gap closes at once. Efficiency also constrains managers: in an efficient market, genuine news affects prices, cosmetic accounting changes do not, and a figure reported only in a footnote is still reflected in the price.

Three Degrees of Efficiency

How much information is already in the price? The standard taxonomy distinguishes three nested degrees of efficiency.

Figure 8.2. The three forms of market efficiency. Each form subsumes the one inside it: strong-form implies semi-strong, which implies weak-form efficiency.

Strong-form efficiency holds that all information, even private, is already in the price — a demanding benchmark few accept. Semi-strong-form holds that all public information is reflected: annual reports, news, analyst opinion. Most believe the U.S. equity market is close to this standard, although the boundary of the definition is unclear: is a document public the instant it is posted online? Weak-form efficiency, the mildest claim, holds only that past prices cannot be used to earn excess profits. Each form contains the ones below it: past prices are themselves public, so strong implies semi-strong implies weak.

Key Concept: Three Forms of the EMH

Weak form: past prices cannot predict future returns. Semi-strong form: all public information is reflected in prices. Strong form: even private information is reflected. Each form implies all the weaker forms below it. Regnault’s “value is determined by the price itself” is weak-form efficiency; his “only a secret pays” is the claim that the market is not strong-form efficient.

III. The Random Walk

Regnault’s second idea followed from his first. If the crowd’s trading has already incorporated all public information into the price, then only new information can change it — and new information, by definition, is unpredictable. At any instant, he reasoned, a price is as likely to rise as to fall, like a coin toss, because if the odds were unequal, arbitrage (his word) would equalize them. Successive changes are therefore independent, and past prices contain no usable information about future prices. The resulting path of prices over time is a random walk. He illustrated the wisdom of the crowd with a simple analogy.

Regnault’s Crowd Estimate of a Building’s Height

Regnault’s illustration is a crowd asked to judge the height of a tall building that none of them may approach:

“… if a large number of people, placed at a certain distance from a high building which they could not approach, were made responsible for approximately measuring with the naked eye its height from base to summit … by adding up all of the collected evaluations and dividing by the number of participants, we get an average height, differing from the actual height only by an average deviation, which would decrease the larger the number of observers, in proportion to the square root of this increase.”

— Jules Regnault, Calcul des chances et philosophie de la bourse (1863). The crowd’s averaged estimate converges on the true value, and its error falls as the square root of the number of observers — the same √ law that governs prices. A few pages later, Regnault turns the observers into speculators: they divide into bulls and bears, and their average estimate becomes the price.

From independence Regnault drew a sharp, testable prediction. If each interval adds an independent random change to the price, the spread of prices around their starting point widens in proportion to the square root of time, because the variances of independent changes add while their standard deviations do not. In his own capitals: the deviation of prices is in direct relation to the square root of time. The square-root law is characteristic of the random walk; Louis Bachelier would rediscover the same √time law in 1900, and physicists would later apply it to Brownian motion.

Regnault then tested the prediction against data, which almost no one in his century had done for a market. Using monthly prices of the French 3% rente from 1825 to 1862, he measured the typical price deviation over a month and over a year, and found that their ratio was very close to the √time ratio the theory predicts.

Horizon: 12 months

Figure 8.3. Regnault’s square-root-of-time law. If price changes are independent, the typical deviation of the price from its starting point grows as $\sqrt{\text{time}}$. Regnault’s two measured deviations of the 3% rente — about 2.73 over a month and 9.50 over a year — fall almost exactly on the curve $2.73\sqrt{\text{months}}$: the law predicts $2.73\times\sqrt{12} = 9.46$, against his observed 9.50. Slide the horizon to read the predicted deviation. After W. N. Goetzmann, “Pre-history of Efficient Market Theory,” from Regnault (1863).

“Is there,” Regnault asked, “a better example of the harmony of theory and experience?” The close fit is, as far as is known, the first empirical support for the random walk and the earliest test of the efficient-market hypothesis, conducted ninety years before the phrase existed.

Regnault also drew a sobering corollary. The fair game is fair only before costs. Each trade pays a brokerage fee, so the expected profit of a round trip, zero before costs, is negative after them. Because the speculator’s edge grows only as √time while the fees accumulate trade by trade, frequent short-term trading tends steadily toward loss — Regnault estimated that a heavy speculator would be wiped out, on average, within about forty trades. Patient long-term investment, he concluded, is a different activity from short-term speculation, a distinction consistent with the fund scorecard at the end of this chapter.

Like Bachelier after him, Regnault was forgotten for the better part of a century. He was arguably the first to state the efficient-market idea clearly and to test it against data, and he drew from it conclusions unfavorable to the short-term speculator.

Fama’s Regressions and the Modern Evidence

Regnault’s √time law was the first test of this kind; the modern evidence for efficiency comes from regressions. In the 1960s Eugene Fama and others ran the test Regnault could not, correlating each day’s return with the returns before it across thousands of stocks. The estimated serial correlations were statistically indistinguishable from zero, so past returns had no detectable relation to future returns. The result is consistent with weak-form efficiency, now supported by a much larger body of data.

Fama’s other major contribution used a different method. With Lawrence Fisher, Michael Jensen, and Richard Roll (1969), he conducted the first formal event study, the same method applied above to mergers, using the 940 stock splits in the record from 1926 to 1960. Aligning every split on its own month 0 and averaging the abnormal returns around it produced a figure that is widely reproduced as evidence of market efficiency.

Figure 8.4. Prices rise before the split and remain flat afterward. Redrawn after Fama, Fisher, Jensen & Roll (1969), Fig. 2b: the cumulative average residual return of 940 NYSE stocks in the months around a split (month 0 = the split). It climbs steeply beforehand, then goes flat — in their words, it rises “dramatically up to the split month, but there is almost no further systematic movement thereafter.”

What Market Efficiency Claims

A split is cosmetic: it increases the number of shares without changing the value of the firm. The run-up beforehand is associated with the tendency of firms to split when they are prospering and about to raise dividends: FFJR found that 71.5% of splits (672 of 940) were followed by dividend increases larger than the average NYSE stock’s. The evidence is consistent with the market interpreting the split as a credible signal of a coming dividend increase and raising the price in anticipation.

The principal lesson is the flat line after month 0. By the time the split occurs, the expected dividend news is already in the price, and there is no systematic drift left to trade on. In FFJR’s words, the market “not only makes good forecasts of the dividend implications of a split, but these forecasts are fully impounded into the price… by the end of the split month.” Some splits disappoint, and efficiency allows for that outcome: it implies that prices are right on average, so that the pre-split price is an unbiased reflection of the probability of the increase. Across 940 splits, the gains and the disappointments offset one another, producing the flat line that corresponds to no abnormal return after public information.

Some of the most informative evidence comes from episodes that appear to contradict efficiency: bubbles. If any pattern should be exploitable, it is the industry that has just doubled or just halved. Robin Greenwood, Andrei Shleifer, and Yang You examined this question in modern U.S. data, tracking what becomes of an industry after a sharp run-up or run-down. In later work, using a database of industry returns reaching back to the nineteenth century, we tested their finding out of sample, on a century of earlier history not covered by the original data.

How you use it

Use it as a check before trading on a story. Ask what the price already reflects: if the market has already incorporated the news, the average path ahead is the same whether the recent history looks like a boom or a crash. The divergence occurs before the event, so an investor should not pay for a forecast that the price already contains.

Testing the Three Forms

Merger announcements are a natural experiment on strong-form efficiency. Insiders — lawyers, bankers, managers — often know before the public, and prices frequently drift up before an official announcement, consistent with private information leaking into the price (and with some of the trading behind it being illegal). The large jump on the announcement day, however, suggests that the leakage is incomplete and that the market is not fully strong-form efficient, as Regnault argued about the value of a secret.

For semi-strong efficiency, the evidence suggests a good first approximation with persistent exceptions. Money managers study public information closely yet, as a group, show no consistent risk-adjusted advantage. Backtests do turn up apparent anomalies — the “Dogs of the Dow,” post-earnings-announcement drift — but trading costs and thin liquidity tend to eliminate the paper profits, and few investors sustain them.

For weak-form efficiency, the evidence is more complicated than the early researchers hoped. There is faint short-run momentum (days), reversals at a few weeks, and slow mean-reversion over years — Fama and French found four-year returns reverting toward their long-run means. The cycles are long and the statistics noisy, however, so trading on them profitably is difficult. Charles Henry Dow tried to identify the same long swings a century ago; today’s hedge funds attempt it with neural networks. If such methods were reliably profitable, it is unclear why their developers would sell the software.

The evidence on patterns is consistent with Regnault’s view: for widely traded securities, prices are close to a random walk, and departures from it would tend to be eliminated by arbitrageurs who buy underpriced securities and short overpriced ones.

The Scorecard: Active versus Passive

A more direct test of efficiency is the track record of the professionals paid to beat the market. If prices contained many exploitable patterns, skilled managers would exploit them, and active funds would routinely outperform a simple index. The evidence suggests that they do not. The standard scorecard — S&P’s SPIVA study, which measures active funds against their benchmarks — shows a consistent pattern.

Figure 8.6. The active-versus-passive scorecard. The share of actively managed U.S. large-cap funds that failed to beat the S&P 500, by horizon. Over a single year most already trail the index; over twenty years more than nine in ten do. The share of funds trailing the index generally rises with the horizon, consistent with market efficiency. Figures approximate, from S&P’s SPIVA U.S. scorecard (year-end); the study is refreshed twice a year.

Over a single year, roughly six in ten active large-cap funds trail the S&P 500; stretch the horizon to twenty years and the figure rises above ninety percent. The pattern is consistent with Regnault’s corollary on a large scale, and it corresponds to William Sharpe’s “arithmetic of active management.” Since all investors together constitute the market, the average actively managed dollar must earn the market return before costs, and therefore must fall short of it after fees. For the average actively managed dollar, beating the index net of costs is arithmetically impossible. For most investors the rational choice is to buy the market.

Competition among traders has also increased. The information a price aggregates once came from human analysts alone; today it also comes from algorithms and machine-learning models that trade in microseconds. To beat the market, an investor must now outperform both the human traders and these machines. The efficient-market hypothesis implies that the market price reflects more information than any single investor is likely to possess.

IV. Regnault and the Arbitrage Pricing Theory

A final historical point concerns the Arbitrage Pricing Theory of Chapter VI, Stephen Ross’s alternative to the CAPM. The theory relies only on the absence of arbitrage: if two portfolios had the same risk but sold at different prices, arbitrageurs would trade until the price difference disappeared, so in equilibrium the difference cannot exist. From that assumption Ross derived a pricing theory that admits many sources of systematic risk and does not require the CAPM’s additional assumptions about utility curves, aggregate holdings, and identical beliefs. The central idea of the theory is Regnault’s: speculators seek out information and act on it quickly, and prices adjust to reflect it.

Ross had never heard of Regnault, who by 1976 had been forgotten for a century. Regnault’s insight is consistent with the modern theory of arbitrage, but it had no influence on the theory’s development. The case parallels that of Bachelier and Black–Scholes: a nineteenth-century figure reached a result that twentieth-century researchers would rediscover independently and develop further, without his knowing that he had come first.

V. Conclusion

Efficient-market theory is a close approximation for liquid, well-regulated markets. How quickly does information get incorporated into prices? The evidence suggests that it is incorporated quickly, because arbitrageurs continually collect, process, and trade on it. Efficiency is also a public good: it keeps prices close to economic value, lowers the cost of judging what a security is worth, and allows ordinary investors to trade with confidence. Speculators seeking profit contribute to that efficiency without intending to do so.

Key Concept: The Efficient Markets Hypothesis

In liquid, well-regulated markets with many informed participants, prices rapidly reflect available information. Markets are not perfectly efficient, but U.S. equity markets are efficient enough that consistently beating them after costs is extremely difficult. The EMH is a useful working assumption and an approximate description of reality. Regnault first described it on the floor of the Paris Bourse, and the fund scorecard, a century and a half later, is consistent with it.
