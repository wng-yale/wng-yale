Chapter IX

The Option Payoff

The hockey stick: contingent claims, the kink at the strike, and the options embedded in many financial contracts.

Introduction

Several of this book's principal pictures concern the value of holding an asset — a dollar compounding, a set of securities and its efficient frontier, the market pricing the risk that cannot be diversified away. This chapter considers a different kind of asset: a claim whose value depends on the price of another asset. An option's value depends on a single number — the strike — and its payoff has a distinctive shape that recurs in many areas of finance: in a homeowner's insurance policy, in the equity of a leveraged firm, in a film studio's sequel rights, in the choice to walk away from a mortgage.

The central figure of the chapter is the payoff diagram, which traders call the “hockey stick” because of its shape. The horizontal axis shows the price of the underlying asset at expiration; the vertical axis shows what the option pays. For a call option — the right, but not the obligation, to buy at the strike — the line is flat at zero below the strike and then rises at forty-five degrees above it. That single kink is the essential feature. Below the strike the right to buy is worthless; above it, every dollar the asset gains adds a dollar to the payoff. As with the other principal figures, I discuss this one in four parts: the logic, changing the inputs, how you use it, and, for readers who want it, the mathematics.

I. The Logic: A Contingent Claim

Begin with the contract. A European call option gives its owner the right to buy one share of an underlying asset at a fixed price — the strike, written $K$ — on a fixed future date, the expiration. The owner pays for this right up front; that payment is the premium. The owner holds a right and has no obligation to exercise it. If exercising is unprofitable, the owner lets the option expire, and the loss is limited to the premium.

Let $S$ denote the price of the underlying at expiration. What is the call worth at that moment? If $S$ is above the strike, the owner exercises: buy at $K$, sell at $S$, and receive $S - K$. If $S$ is at or below the strike, exercising would mean paying $K$ for something worth less, so the owner declines and the option expires worthless. The payoff is therefore

Call Option Payoff at Expiration

$$\text{Payoff} = \max(S - K,\ 0)$$

The $\max$ operator, which takes the larger of the two values, is the characteristic feature of optionality and produces the kink. A stock's payoff is a straight diagonal line: each dollar of value adds a dollar to the payoff. A bond's payoff (absent default) is a flat line: you get the promised amount whatever happens. The option is the first payoff in this book with a bend, and the main properties of options follow from that bend.

Key Idea: Intrinsic Value and the Kink

The quantity $\max(S-K,0)$ is the option's intrinsic value — what it would be worth if expiration were right now. The bend occurs at $S = K$. To the left, the call is out of the money and intrinsically worthless; to the right, it is in the money and worth $S-K$; at the strike it is at the money. The kink marks the point where exercising the right becomes worthwhile.

The Premium and the Break-Even

The payoff diagram and the profit diagram are two different pictures, and confusing them is a common beginner's error. The payoff is what the option pays at expiration; the profit is that payoff net of the premium you paid to acquire it. Because the premium is a sunk cost, subtracting it lowers the entire payoff line by the amount of the premium. The option now begins to pay before you profit: you break even only once the asset has climbed far enough above the strike to recover the premium. If you paid a premium $P$ for a call struck at $K$, your break-even price is $K + P$, and below it — even when the option finishes in the money — you have still lost money on the round trip.

Notice what the shape guarantees. Your loss is capped at the premium, no matter how far the asset falls; your gain is, in principle, unbounded, rising dollar for dollar as the asset price rises. This asymmetry — small, known, limited downside; large, unknown, open-ended upside — explains why options are widely used for both speculation and insurance.

Put Options

A put option is the counterpart of the call: the right to sell at the strike. Its owner profits when the asset price falls, since the right to sell at $K$ becomes valuable precisely when the market price $S$ has dropped below $K$. Its payoff is

Put Option Payoff at Expiration

$$\text{Payoff} = \max(K - S,\ 0)$$

The put's payoff diagram is the call's reflected about a vertical line: high on the left, sloping down to the strike, then flat at zero to the right. For puts the insurance analogy is exact. A put on a stock you own is an insurance policy on that stock: pay a premium today, and if the price collapses, the put pays you the difference, compensating your losses below $K$. The strike is the deductible level; the premium is the insurance premium; the expiration is the term of the policy. Homeowners who buy insurance hold options under another name.

Use it to design an exposure. Add these payoff lines together to build the outcome you want: limit a loss with a protective put, finance it by selling upside, or replicate one security from a combination of others. When two positions have the same kinked payoff line, they must cost the same today; this principle is the starting point of option pricing.

II. Changing the Inputs

The payoff at expiration is fixed geometry — a kink at $K$ and two straight arms. An option is rarely held to the last instant, however, and its value before expiration is more complex. The central visual fact of option pricing is that the value curve of an unexpired option is a smooth, rounded version of the kinked payoff and lies above the payoff. This section describes how the smooth curve changes as the inputs change.

Time Value: The Effects of Volatility and Time

Why does the value curve lie above the payoff? Before expiration the future price is uncertain, and the asymmetry of the option gives that uncertainty value. Consider a call struck at $K$ with the asset price exactly at the money, $S = K$. Its intrinsic value is zero, since the payoff is zero at the kink. Yet the option is plainly worth something, because between now and expiration the asset might rise, and if it does the owner keeps the upside, while if it falls the owner loses nothing more. The chance of a favorable move has positive value and the chance of an unfavorable one costs nothing extra. That surplus — the height of the smooth curve above the kink — is the option's time value.

Two inputs determine the height of the curve above the payoff:

  • Volatility. A more volatile underlying makes large price changes more likely in both directions, but only the favorable direction adds to the option's payoff. Greater volatility therefore raises the entire value curve, and the sharp corner is replaced by a broader curve. This property is one of the most important and least intuitive facts about options: an option on a more volatile asset is worth more, because the owner is protected from the additional downside but retains the additional upside. Higher uncertainty reduces the value of many risky assets and increases the value of an option.
  • Time to expiry. Time to expiry has a similar effect. A longer horizon allows the asset price to vary more, so a longer-dated option has more time value. As expiration approaches, the curve falls toward the kinked payoff; at expiration, time value is zero and the value curve coincides with the payoff. Options are wasting assets: their value declines toward intrinsic value as expiration approaches.

Key Concept: Moneyness and the Three Regions

The position of the asset price relative to the strike determines how the option behaves. In the money ($S > K$ for a call): the option has intrinsic value and behaves increasingly like the stock itself — the value curve runs nearly parallel to the payoff's upper arm. At the money ($S \approx K$): all value is time value, and the option is at its most sensitive to volatility — the gap between value and payoff is largest at the kink. Out of the money ($S < K$ for a call): no intrinsic value, only the possibility of finishing in the money; the curve lies just above zero, reflecting a small probability of a payoff.

Changing the Strike

Changing the strike changes the location of the kink along the horizontal axis. A lower-struck call is more likely to finish in the money, so it is worth more and commands a higher premium; a higher-struck call is cheaper to buy but less likely to pay. This is the same trade-off a shopper faces with an insurance deductible: a policy that pays out sooner (a higher put strike, a lower call strike) costs more up front. The strike determines how much protection, or how much leverage, the buyer obtains.

Steepness and Leverage

A further property explains much of the appeal of options to speculators. Because a call can be bought for a small premium yet changes in value nearly dollar-for-dollar with the stock once it is in the money, a modest sum controls a large exposure. A 10% rise in a stock might double the value of an at-the-money call. This embedded leverage is the counterpart of the capped downside: the buyer risks a small amount to obtain a large exposure, and the shape of the payoff makes this exposure possible without borrowing.

Figure 9.2. The value of a call before maturity. The bold line is the payoff at expiration, $\max(S-K,0)$. The smooth curve above it is what the option is worth today, with time still left to run: it is strictly positive even at the strike, where the intrinsic value is zero, because the stock might yet rise. The gap between the curve and the kinked payoff is time value. As time to maturity approaches zero, the curve converges to the payoff; adding time or volatility raises the curve. The function that draws this curve is the Black–Scholes–Merton formula.

III. How You Use It

The payoff diagram is a tool for decisions. Practitioners use options for insurance and for income, and the payoff diagram also identifies options embedded in claims that are not called options.

Insurance: The Protective Put

Return to the homeowner. An investor who owns a stock but fears a crash can buy a put struck below the current price. The combined position — stock plus put — has a payoff that is flat on the downside (the put makes up any loss below the strike) and rising on the upside (the stock gains in value, and the put expires worthless). The position is the simplest form of portfolio insurance: a floor under your losses, bought for a premium, with the upside left intact. Its payoff diagram is a call payoff raised by the amount of the strike, with a guaranteed minimum equal to the strike.

Income: The Covered Call

The inverse trade is the covered call: own the stock and write (sell) a call against it, collecting the premium. You have sold away the upside above the strike in exchange for cash today. The payoff rises with the stock up to the strike and then flattens: the writer gives up gains above the strike and receives premium income. Some endowments and pension funds run covered-call programs for this reason: in a flat or gently rising market, the premiums add to returns. In effect, the covered writer occupies the position of the insurance company: the writer collects premiums and bears the risk of large price increases.

Equity as a Call Option on the Firm

The idea that equity is a call option on the firm, part of the work for which Robert Merton and Myron Scholes received the Nobel Prize, is central to the modern theory of corporate finance. Consider a firm financed with both debt and equity. The debtholders are promised a fixed face amount $F$ when the debt matures. What happens at maturity depends on the value $V$ of the firm's assets:

  • If the firm is worth more than the debt ($V > F$), the shareholders pay off the debt and keep the remainder, $V - F$.
  • If the firm is worth less than the debt ($V < F$), the shareholders, protected by limited liability, hand the firm over to the creditors and walk away with nothing.

Consider these outcomes with the payoff formula in mind. The shareholders receive $\max(V - F,\ 0)$. The equity of a levered firm is a call option on the firm's assets, struck at the face value of the debt. The shareholders own the upside; the creditors have, in effect, sold them that call — or equivalently, the creditors own the firm outright but have written a put to the shareholders (the option to "put" the firm to the lenders in default). This representation is the Merton model of credit risk. The model is used to price corporate debt and to estimate default probabilities, and it implies that shareholders of a distressed firm may prefer risky gambles: more volatility raises the value of their call, even as it harms the bondholders. The call option payoff is embedded in the firm's capital structure.

Options are embedded in insurance, in employment contracts, in the choice to expand a factory, in a patent, and in the ability to abandon a failing project. The payoff diagram makes these options visible.

IV. The Mathematics (optional)

The three preceding sections do not depend on this material. What follows is for readers who want to see the formal logic that connects the results. Two results are central: put–call parity, which follows almost entirely from arithmetic, and the Black–Scholes formula, one of the major results of twentieth-century economics.

Derivation: Put–Call Parity

Put–call parity is a relationship that the prices of a call and a put must satisfy if no riskless arbitrage exists; it does not depend on any pricing model. It relates four instruments: a call and a put on the same asset, both struck at $K$ with the same expiration $T$; the underlying asset itself, priced $S$; and a riskless bond.

Consider two portfolios held to expiration.

  • Portfolio A: one call (price $C$) plus cash equal to the present value of the strike, $K e^{-rT}$, invested at the riskless rate $r$. At expiration the cash grows to exactly $K$.
  • Portfolio B: one put (price $P$) plus one share of the underlying (price $S$).

Now compare their values at expiration, in the two possible states:

  • If $S_T \ge K$: Portfolio A's call is worth $S_T - K$, plus the $K$ in cash, for $S_T$ in all. Portfolio B's put expires worthless, leaving just the share, worth $S_T$. The two values are equal.
  • If $S_T < K$: Portfolio A's call expires worthless, leaving the $K$ in cash. Portfolio B's put is worth $K - S_T$, plus the share worth $S_T$, for $K$ in all. The two values are equal.

The two portfolios produce identical payoffs in every state of the world, so they must cost the same today — otherwise one could buy the cheaper and sell the dearer for a certain profit. Setting today's prices equal gives the parity relation:

$$C + K e^{-rT} = P + S \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad C - P = S - K e^{-rT}.$$

The relation is derived without any assumption about how the asset price evolves. It implies that, given the price of either a call or a put, together with the stock price and the interest rate, one can determine the price of the other. It also makes precise the earlier claim that a protective put (own the share, buy the put — the left side rearranged) has the same payoff as holding a call plus cash: in algebraic terms, the insured position and the call-plus-cash position are equivalent.

The Black–Scholes Formula and Its Interpretation

Parity relates a call to a put but does not, by itself, tell you what either is worth. For that we need a model of how the underlying moves. Black, Scholes, and Merton supplied one in 1973, assuming the asset price follows a continuous random walk (geometric Brownian motion) with constant volatility $\sigma$. I omit the differential equation and present the formula directly, because each of its terms has an economic interpretation. For a European call with no dividends,

$$C = \underbrace{S\,N(d_1)}_{\text{value of the shares you might get}} \;-\; \underbrace{K e^{-rT} N(d_2)}_{\text{present value of what you'd pay}},$$ $$d_1 = \frac{\ln(S/K) + \left(r + \tfrac12\sigma^2\right)T}{\sigma\sqrt{T}}, \qquad d_2 = d_1 - \sigma\sqrt{T},$$

where $N(\cdot)$ is the cumulative standard-normal distribution — the same bell-curve area that produced Value at Risk in Chapter III. The structure parallels the two portfolios of put–call parity. The formula says: the call is worth the shares you stand to receive, weighted by $N(d_1)$, minus the strike you would have to pay, discounted and weighted by $N(d_2)$.

The two weights have direct interpretations:

  • $N(d_2)$ is (in the risk-neutral world) the probability the option finishes in the money — the chance you exercise at all. It scales the strike you expect to pay.
  • $N(d_1)$ is the option's delta — the sensitivity of the option's value to a one-dollar move in the stock, and the number of shares you would hold to hedge the option. It scales the shares you expect to receive. Deep in the money $N(d_1)\to 1$ and the call tracks the stock one-for-one; deep out, $N(d_1)\to 0$ and the value of the call barely changes.

Each effect described in Section II is represented in the formula. Raise $\sigma$ or $T$ and $d_1, d_2$ spread apart, raising $C$ — the rounding of the kink. Let $T\to 0$ and the formula reduces to $\max(S-K,0)$, so that the smooth curve coincides with the payoff. The principal contribution of Black and Scholes was the derivation of the formula: by continuously trading $N(d_1)$ shares against the option, one can build a riskless hedge, and a riskless position can only earn the riskless rate. The option's price is the price at which that no-arbitrage argument holds, and for this reason the expected return of the stock does not appear in the formula.

Sidebar: A Short History of Options and Their Pricing

Options are much older than the mathematics used to price them. Aristotle tells the story of the philosopher Thales, who, foreseeing a good olive harvest, paid deposits for the right to use every olive press in Miletus — a call option on pressing capacity — and profited when the harvest came in. Options on tulip bulbs traded in seventeenth-century Amsterdam; puts and calls on shares changed hands in London coffee-houses and on the curb outside the Paris Bourse.

Despite this trading, there was no method for determining what an option was worth. The closest thing to a method came from Henri Lefèvre, private financial secretary to the House of Rothschild, who in the 1870s devised graphical ways of charting option positions — precursors of the payoff diagrams in this chapter. Lefèvre’s diagrams represented option positions, but he did not price them.

Lefèvre’s option diagram, 1873. A compound option position on French bonds — settlement price along the horizontal axis, gain or loss along the vertical, the bold line tracing the payoff. Drawn a century before options had a pricing theory, the diagram is a precursor of those in this chapter, comparable in this respect to Markowitz’s first hand-drawn frontier. (Lefèvre, Traité des valeurs mobilières et des opérations de Bourse, 1873; public domain.)

The first serious attempt to price options came from a doctoral student. In 1900 Louis Bachelier submitted a thesis, Théorie de la spéculation, that modeled the Paris Bourse's price movements as Brownian motion — five years before Einstein used the same mathematics for pollen grains — and derived option values from it. His work was mostly forgotten for half a century, dismissed as neither pure mathematics nor respectable economics.

The pricing problem was solved in 1973, when Fischer Black and Myron Scholes published their formula, with Robert Merton supplying the continuous-hedging argument that made the derivation rigorous; the Chicago Board Options Exchange opened in the same year. Scholes and Merton received the Nobel Prize in 1997; Black had died two years earlier and could not share it. Within a decade the formula was programmed into traders’ calculators, and a contract traded for at least three centuries had a pricing theory.

Figure 9.3. A firm’s securities as options on its assets. Read the horizontal axis as the total value $V$ of the firm at the moment its debt comes due. The shareholders hold a call on the assets struck at the face value of the debt $F$: worthless in default, rising dollar-for-dollar once the firm clears its obligations. The creditors hold the mirror piece, $\min(V,F)$ — a riskless bond minus a put — and the two always sum to the whole firm. Raising the asset volatility displays the conflict of interest: more risk raises the value of the shareholders’ call and lowers the value of the debt, although the value of the firm is unchanged.

V. Summary

The option payoff is one of the book's principal pictures and the first with a bend. Its single kink at the strike — produced by the operator $\max(S-K,0)$ — represents an asymmetry found in many areas of finance: limited, known downside and open-ended upside, bought for a premium paid today. The other results of the chapter follow from that shape. Subtracting the premium converts payoff into profit and fixes the break-even. A put has the mirrored shape, and a put is economically equivalent to insurance.

Before expiration the kinked payoff becomes a smooth value curve that lies above the payoff, and the height of the rounding — the option's time value — grows with volatility and with time, then falls to zero at expiration. Black, Scholes, and Merton derived that value curve in 1973, solving a problem that had been open since the Paris Bourse of the 1860s. The same shape appears in contracts that are not labeled options: the equity of a levered firm is itself a call on the firm's assets. Homeowners who buy flood insurance, venture capitalists who stage their investments, and shareholders of distressed companies hold options, whether or not they use the term.

Key Concept: What the Payoff Picture Teaches

An option converts an uncertain future into an asymmetric claim: you keep the favorable outcomes and are shielded from the unfavorable ones, in exchange for a premium. The kink at the strike separates the two regimes. Before expiration the option's value lies above the payoff by an amount that grows with volatility and time; for this reason, unlike most assets in this book, an option is worth more when its underlying is more uncertain.
